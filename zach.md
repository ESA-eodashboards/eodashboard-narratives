## Results

&emsp;Analysis of the diagrams and comparison between the cold and warm months indicate that the highest risk occurs during winter, when NO<sub>2</sub> concentrations are elevated. This seasonal pattern is closely associated with the photochemical processes involved in ozone formation. NO2 undergoes photolysis under the influence of solar radiation, leading to lower concentrations during periods characterized by stronger solar irradiance and higher surface temperatures. Consequently, during winter, NO2 has a longer atmospheric lifetime because both solar radiation and temperature remain relatively low.

&emsp;A notable difference between the two seasons is observed during weekends. In winter, the reduced levels of solar radiation and lower temperatures slow the photolysis of atmospheric NO<sub>2</sub>, resulting in higher background concentrations. In contrast, during summer, the greater solar energy promotes faster NO2 photolysis, which leads to substantially lower concentrations on Saturday compared with Friday.

&emsp;When comparing the days of the week separately for each area, Thursday is identified as the day with the highest NO2 concentrations in both seasonal periods. NO<sub>2</sub> emitted directly or formed in the atmosphere during the working week may accumulate because it is not removed instantaneously. As a result, concentrations reach their maximum on Thursday, likely due to the combined effects of increased traffic-related emissions and intense industrial activity. On Friday, as industrial activity decreases and road traffic is reduced because of residents leaving the city and the increased prevalence of remote working, NO2 concentrations begin to decline. However, they remain higher than weekend values due to the persistence of background concentrations.

&emsp;Regarding the spatial distribution of pollutants, the highest concentrations are observed in the central urban core of Milan, which can primarily be attributed to intense traffic activity and the presence of significant industrial emission sources. Elevated values are also detected in the northern and, especially, northwestern part of the study area. This spatial differentiation appears to be linked to the geomorphological characteristics of the region, as the Alpine mountain range extends to the north and northwest of Milan. The presence of these mountainous barriers may restrict atmospheric dispersion and favor the accumulation of air masses enriched in NO<sub>2</sub> originating from emissions within the metropolitan area. In contrast, south of Milan, where major orographic barriers are absent, atmospheric pollutants can disperse more effectively, resulting in lower local concentrations.

&emsp;This interpretation is consistent with the established meteorological controls on air pollution in Milan and the wider Po Valley. During the October 2024 - April 2025 period, pollutant dispersion was likely influenced by the combined effects of weak ventilation, frequent atmospheric stagnation, temperature inversions, and the basin-like morphology of the region, which is bounded by the Alps and the Apennines. Under such conditions, NO<sub>2</sub> emitted from traffic and industrial sources within the Milan metropolitan area can persist in the lower atmosphere and accumulate preferentially in sectors where horizontal and vertical dispersion are constrained. The higher concentrations observed in the northern and northwestern parts of the study area are therefore physically plausible, particularly when easterly or southeasterly transport, or stagnant wind conditions, favor the displacement and retention of polluted air masses toward the Alpine foothills. Nevertheless, this interpretation should be supported by a detailed wind-field analysis for the examined period to confirm the frequency and persistence of the relevant transport patterns.
